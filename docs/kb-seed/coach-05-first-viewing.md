_Starter document written to give the Coach something to answer with. Derek should review and
correct every claim before agents rely on it._

## The day before

**Confirm.** A short message the day before, naming the time and the meeting point. This one
habit prevents more no-shows than anything else you will do.

**Check availability again.** Units get booked between arranging a viewing and holding it.
Turning up to show something that sold yesterday damages trust in a way that is hard to
recover.

**Re-read what they told you.** Their purpose, their budget range, what they reacted to in
earlier conversations. Walking in without that is how you end up pitching a two-bedroom to
someone who told you last week they need three.

**Prepare two or three units, not one.** People choose by comparison. A single unit gives them
nothing to judge it against, and they will go and find the comparison elsewhere.

## Arriving

Get there early. Being there first, knowing where to park and which lift lobby to use, does
more for your credibility than anything you say.

Meet them at a specific, findable spot. "The sales gallery" is not a meeting point in a
development with three entrances.

Start with a short orientation before going into the unit: what phase this is, what is
completed, what the surroundings will look like when the rest is built. Two minutes of context
changes how they read everything they are about to see.

## During the viewing

**Let them look before you talk.** The instinct is to narrate. Resist it. Give them the first
minute in the unit in silence — where they go and what they touch tells you more than any
question.

**Answer what they ask, not what you prepared.** A rehearsed feature list delivered over
someone's actual question is the clearest signal that you are selling rather than helping.

**Be straight about the drawbacks.** Every unit has them — the afternoon sun, the lift
distance, the view that will change when the next tower goes up. Raise them yourself. A client
who discovers a drawback after you hid it stops believing the good things you said too.

**Never guess.** On price, availability, maintenance fees, completion dates, bumiputera quota
or foreign-buyer eligibility — if you are not certain, say you will confirm and come back the
same day. These are the facts a client makes a financial decision on. A confident wrong answer
here is far worse than a short delay.

**Watch the second person.** If they came as a couple or with family, the quieter one is often
the decision-maker. Address both.

## Closing the viewing

Ask what they thought, and then be quiet long enough for a real answer. The pause is
uncomfortable and it is where the useful information comes out.

If they liked it, ask what would need to be true for them to move forward. That question
surfaces the real obstacle — usually financing, timing, or a person who is not in the room.

Agree a specific next step before you part, the same as with a walk-in. A date, a document,
a comparison unit.

## After

**Write your notes the same day.** What they reacted to, what they dismissed, what they
compared it against, who else was involved, and what you promised to send. This is the most
valuable record you will ever keep on a client, and it is unrecoverable by tomorrow.

**Send what you promised that evening.** Nothing erodes a good viewing faster than a document
that arrives three days late.

**Follow up on the obstacle, not the unit.** If financing was the issue, the useful follow-up
is about financing. Re-sending the floor plan of a unit they have already seen is not a
follow-up.

## If it does not go well

Some viewings end with a clear no. That is a good outcome if you learn why. Ask, record it,
and use it — a client who says "too far from my mother's place" has just told you the single
most important thing about their next viewing.

Do not argue with a no and do not immediately propose another unit in the same breath. Take
the answer, thank them, and come back with something that addresses what they actually said.
