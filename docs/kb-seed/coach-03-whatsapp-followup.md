## Why WhatsApp is different

On a portal, you are one of several agents answering the same enquiry. On WhatsApp, you are in
someone's personal messaging app, sitting between their family group and their friends. That
buys you attention and it costs you patience. People reply faster and they also mute faster.

Two consequences. Write the way you would speak, because a formal broadcast tone stands out
badly in that context. And treat every message as costing you something — you have a limited
number before you become the contact they stop opening.

## Cadence

There is no universal schedule, but a workable default after first contact is: reply the same
day, follow up the next day if they went quiet mid-conversation, then space it out — a few
days, then about a week, then a final message. Roughly four or five touches over two to three
weeks, then stop.

The rule that matters more than the schedule: **every follow-up must carry something new.**
A new unit that matches what they said. A change in availability. A genuine update on a
project they asked about. If you have nothing new, you do not have a follow-up — you have a
nudge, and nudges are what get you muted.

"Just following up" is the single most common message in this business and it almost never
works.

## Length and formatting

Short messages, one idea each. Two or three lines is normal. If you need more than that, it is
probably a call or a document, not a WhatsApp message.

Avoid walls of text with emoji bullet points. It reads as a broadcast, and people can tell the
difference between a message written for them and a template sent to fifty people.

Never send a voice note unless they have sent you one first. Many people cannot listen to one
at work, and it forces them to deal with you on your schedule rather than theirs.

## Moving from chat to viewing

The conversation has to change gear at some point, and most agents leave it too late. Once you
know their purpose, area and rough timeline, propose a viewing directly.

Offer two specific times rather than asking "when are you free?" — an open question puts the
work on them and usually gets no reply. Two options gets an answer, even if the answer is
"neither, how about Sunday".

Confirm the day before. Confirmations reduce no-shows more than anything else you can do, and
a no-show costs you the slot and the developer's patience.

## Handling silence

Silence is not rejection, but it is information. Someone who replied enthusiastically twice
and then stopped has usually either hit a financing problem or found something else. Both are
worth a direct, low-pressure question rather than another cheerful check-in.

A message that gives them an easy way to say no often gets a reply when nothing else does —
something to the effect of: if the timing is not right, tell me and I will stop messaging, and
you can come back whenever it suits. People appreciate being let off the hook, and a
surprising number use that message to re-engage instead.

## Records

WhatsApp conversations are business records. What you promised in a chat is what the client
will hold you to.

Keep the lead record updated as the conversation develops — do not rely on being able to
scroll back through the thread months later. Note what they said about budget, timeline and
financing when they say it.

Never move a client conversation to a personal number that the agency cannot see. If you leave
or go on holiday, someone else has to be able to pick it up.

## Boundaries

Business hours are a real thing even on a platform that never closes. Late-night messages get
read as pressure. If you write something at midnight, schedule it or send it in the morning.

Do not add clients to broadcast lists or groups without asking. It is the fastest way to be
blocked, and depending on how their number was obtained it can also be a data-protection
problem.

If a client asks you to stop contacting them, stop, and record that they asked. That request
is binding regardless of where they are in the pipeline.
