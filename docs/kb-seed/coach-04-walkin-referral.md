## Walk-ins: the most information, the least time

Someone who walks into a gallery has already spent effort to be there. That makes them warmer
than almost any online lead. It also means you have one conversation to get what you need,
because when they leave, they are gone unless you have a number.

**Get the contact details early, not at the end.** Most agents leave it to the door, and that
is where it gets awkward and where it gets refused. Ask early, naturally, tied to something
useful: you are going to send them the floor plan, or check availability and come back to
them. A reason to exchange numbers is easier to accept than a request at goodbye.

**Watch, do not just ask.** Who did they come with? A couple, a parent, a young family, an
investor on their own — each changes what matters. What do they walk towards in the show unit?
What do they ask about without being prompted? Someone who opens the kitchen cabinets and asks
about storage is telling you something they will never say in a budget question.

**Write it down within the hour.** Walk-in observations are the most valuable and the most
perishable information in the job. By tomorrow you will remember that they seemed keen and
nothing else.

## The qualifying conversation

The same four things you need from any lead — purpose, area, timeline, financing position —
but you can get them conversationally rather than by asking. A walk-in who says "we're
thinking about after the wedding in March" has given you their timeline without being asked.

Two things worth asking directly at a walk-in, because they are hard to get any other way:

- **Have they seen other projects nearby?** Their answer tells you what you are being compared
  against, and what they thought of it.
- **Is anyone else involved in the decision?** A spouse, a parent funding part of it, a
  business partner. Discovering a decision-maker late is one of the most common reasons a
  promising walk-in stalls.

## Before they leave

Agree the next step out loud, and make it specific. "I'll send you the floor plan and the
price list tonight, and I'll follow up on Thursday about the corner unit" is a next step.
"I'll be in touch" is not.

Then actually do it that evening. The gap between the gallery and your first message is where
most walk-in leads are lost.

## Referrals: trust arrives before you do

A referral starts with credibility you did not earn, on loan from whoever passed the name.
That is an advantage and an obligation.

**Acknowledge the referrer.** Open by saying who suggested you get in touch. It places you
instantly and it makes the conversation feel expected rather than cold.

**Thank the referrer, promptly, separately.** Regardless of whether the lead converts. This is
partly courtesy and mostly self-interest: people refer again when they see it landed well.

**Never let a referral go quiet without telling the referrer.** If the lead goes cold, a short
message to the person who referred them protects the relationship. Silence makes them assume
you dropped it.

**Do not assume a referral is pre-qualified.** Warm is not the same as ready. Referrals still
need purpose, area, timeline and financing established. Skipping that because the conversation
feels friendly is a common and expensive mistake.

## Tracking both

Record the source precisely. "Walk-in — Bangsar gallery, Saturday" and "Referral — from
Mr Tan, past client" are both far more useful six months later than "walk-in" and "referral".
Referrals in particular need the referrer's name recorded, because your follow-up obligation
runs to two people, not one.

Log the same fields as any other lead, plus the observations. For a walk-in that means what
they reacted to. For a referral it means what the referrer told you about them before you
made contact.

Set the follow-up before you close the record. A walk-in or referral without a scheduled next
contact is the same as a lead you never captured.

## Where these go wrong

Treating a warm lead as a sure thing and following up less than you would a cold one. It
happens constantly, and it is why referrals are lost more often than they should be.

Not establishing ownership on a referral. If a colleague passed you the name, agree explicitly
who owns the lead before either of you contacts them.

Letting a walk-in leave with a brochure and no exchange of numbers. A brochure costs the
agency money and buys you nothing without a way to follow up.
